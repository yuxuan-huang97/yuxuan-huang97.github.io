## Overview

Research has shown that Virtual Reality nature immersion experience is beneficial for psychological wellbeing in a way similar to real nature exposure. Existing VR nature experiences are typically offered through 360 videos or 3D computer-generated virtual environments.

To better understand the relative pros and cons and gain important insights into the design considerations for these types of VR nature experiences, in this study we focus on a key difference between 360 video and 3D computer graphics. We compare between active locomotion (free exploration afforded by a 3D virtual environment) and passive locomotion (camera following a fixed trajectory similar to pre-recorded 360 videos) in terms of the restorative outcome and user preferences.

As interest rises in the potential therapeutic benefits of virtual nature (VN) exposure, particularly for populations who lack ready access to healthy outdoor environments, it is increasingly important to more clearly elucidate the factors associated with promoting optimal outcomes from these experiences. Currently, most commercial products in this market use 360° video to surround users with photorealistic views of spectacular natural scenes. While recent advances in computer graphics hardware and software have begun to make feasible the modeling and rendering of nearly photorealistic geometrically and botanically correct 3D VN assets, few 3DVN views can yet be mistaken for photographs of real places, and successfully modeling aesthetically-pleasing large scale VN environments remains a painstaking process that requires considerable talent and time. On the other hand, fully 3D virtual environments (VEs) offer fundamentally greater affordances for free exploration than is possible with 360° video technology. 

To help inform the choice between pursuing research towards a better 3DVN vs a better 360° video approach, we focus in this paper on specifically on assessing the extent to which affording active free exploration, versus guided passive exploration, might enhance the restorative potential of a virtual nature immersion experience, as the lack of affordance for arbitrary free exploration is one of the most intractable shortcomings of 360° video-based experiences. Specifically, we report the results of a within-subjects experiment with 20 participants comparing multiple measures of well-being after HMD-based 3DVN immersion under two different conditions of locomotion control: active (using a joystick) and passive (akin to a 360° video viewing experience, in which the user is free to look around but their base position follows a pre-defined path at a pre-defined pace). Each participant experienced both conditions, in similar but distinct, nearly-photorealistic 3D VNEs, on different days in counterbalanced order. To ensure the equivalence of the visual stimulus between the active and passive trials over the set of all participants, the path and pace of each participant’s passive immersion experience was exactly defined by the path and pace recorded from the active control trial of a preceding matched participant. Each virtual nature immersion experience was preceded by an arithmetic stressor task. Comparing baseline, post-stressor and post-VR measures between the active and passive locomotion conditions, we found a significantly greater increase in ZIPERS Positive Affect and a significantly greater decrease in ZIPERS and PANAS Negative Affect after the active vs passive exposure. The active exposure condition was also associated with higher ratings on the Compatibility and Fascination subscales of the Perceived Restorativeness Scale, and 75% of the participants in our exit survey chose the active condition as their overall preferred option.

Altogether, these results suggest that affording opportunities for active locomotion control within a restoratively-aimed virtual nature immersion experience may fundamentally support better wellness outcomes than offering a passive “guided tour”, suggesting inherent intrinsic advantages to a 3DVE over 360° video approach.
